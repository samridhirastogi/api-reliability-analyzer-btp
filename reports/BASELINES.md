## Why a random forest

Logistic regression on the identical feature set, trained on the identical cases, reaches 0.748 against the forest's 0.963. That is a wide gap, and it is the direct answer to "why not something simpler": the signal is not linearly separable in these features. A latency rise *together with* a CPU rise means something different from either on its own, and a linear model cannot represent that interaction.

The single-measurement rules are the more revealing comparison, because they are roughly what a threshold-based alert already does. Ranking APIs by the latency z-score alone scores 0.120 against a floor of 0.085 -- very close to useless on its own. CPU alone does considerably better at 0.623 but still falls far short of the model.

So the ordering is: a single metric is not enough, a linear combination of all of them is not enough either, and the value is in the interactions. That is the case for building a model rather than tuning a threshold.

## How much of the headline number is the labelling scheme

Under the main labelling the forest scores 0.963; under propagation-negative labelling, where services degraded by a fault in a dependency are labelled healthy rather than masked out, it scores 0.935.

**Those two PR-AUCs are not directly comparable**, and the reason matters: PR-AUC is measured against the positive rate, which halves from 0.085 to 0.041 when the propagating windows join the negative class. A random ranker scores 0.085 in the first column and 0.041 in the second, so the columns have different floors. Against their own floors the model is 11x chance under the main labelling and 23x under the alternative.

**The headline number largely survives the harder labelling.** Absolute PR-AUC falls by only 0.027, and against its own floor the model is *better* under the alternative scheme (23x versus 11x), because the alternative asks it to pick 33,525 positives out of a much larger and more confusable pool. The masking decision is therefore not what is carrying the result.

The drop that does exist is the expected one. A service whose latency has doubled because its dependency is failing looks, in its own telemetry, much like a service failing on its own -- and this labelling requires it to be called healthy. The main pipeline masks those windows rather than asserting an answer the benchmark cannot support, so 0.963 is performance on *unambiguous* windows; 0.935 is what happens when the ambiguous ones are forced into the negative class.

Note also that the linear and single-metric baselines degrade much further under the alternative labelling (logistic regression 0.748 -> 0.614, latency z-score 0.120 -> 0.055), so the harder labelling widens the model's advantage rather than narrowing it.

Telling a genuinely failing API apart from one dragged down by a neighbour needs the call graph -- which service called which -- and that is the natural next step for this work.
