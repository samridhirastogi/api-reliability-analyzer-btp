# How far does a fault actually spread?

Generated by `python -m src.propagation`. For every service in every recording, mean p50 latency over the 30-330s *after* the injection is compared with the same service's latency before it.

## The headline

| | Injected service | Every other service |
|---|---|---|
| Service-case pairs | 733 | 9,806 |
| Median latency ratio | 2.613 | **1.006** |
| Share degrading >10% | 88.9% | 22.8% |
| Share degrading >20% | 84.2% | 15.6% |
| Share degrading >50% | 70.4% | 8.8% |
| Share degrading >100% | 57.2% | 6.0% |

**Propagation is real but narrow.** 15.6% of non-injected services degrade by more than 20% while a fault is active; the median one does not move at all (ratio 1.006). The injected service, by contrast, degrades 84.2% of the time.

## Why this matters for labelling

The masking policy treats *every* non-injected service as ambiguous while a fault is active. The measurement above says that is far more conservative than the data warrants:

| | Windows |
|---|---|
| Total (excluding infrastructure) | 816,822 |
| Masked by the policy | 423,686 (51.9%) |
| ...belonging to a service that actually degraded | 81,678 (19.3% of masked) |
| ...clean negatives being discarded | **342,008** |

Masking therefore discards 423,686 windows to guard against an ambiguity that affects 81,678 of them -- roughly 5x more data than the problem justifies. Labelling those services negative instead takes the training set from 393,136 to 735,144 windows (+87%).

That is the evidence behind this project reporting the propagation-negative labelling as its primary result. The masked variant is kept as a comparison, and the gap between them -- see `BASELINES.md` -- bounds what the choice is worth.

## By system

| System | Non-injected services | Degrading >20% |
|---|---|---|
| Online Boutique | 2,187 | 24.4% |
| Sock Shop | 1,625 | 14.2% |
| Train Ticket | 5,994 | 12.8% |

Online Boutique propagates most, which fits its shape: a single `frontend` fans out to every other service, so a fault anywhere has a short path to it. Train Ticket has many more services and longer, narrower call chains, so a given fault reaches proportionally fewer of them.

## What this measurement is not

A latency ratio is evidence that a service degraded, not proof that the injected fault caused it: load varies on its own, and some of these services are noisy. The figure is used to size the labelling problem and to justify a policy, never to label a window -- latency is an input feature, so labelling on it would be circular.

Establishing actual causation needs the call graph, which the benchmark ships for 240 of the recordings. A first look at six of them was inconclusive: the services that transitively call the injected one showed no degradation, while a few that cannot reach it did, and only nine service-level edges were recovered. That is a lead worth following, not a result.
